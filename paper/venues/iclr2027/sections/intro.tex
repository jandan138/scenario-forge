\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

``Open the IKA OVEN~125 and unload the glassware'' is a legal line in a lab
notebook. It is not a scene. Before a robot can rehearse it, the oven needs a
door on a hinge, a handle a gripper can close on, shelves that count as
placement surfaces, and a laboratory floor for a Lift2 to stand on. The
protocol names the instrument; the simulator needs its USD.

Two lines of work approach that gap from opposite sides. Real-to-sim systems
reconstruct a workspace that already exists, from a scan or a photograph
\citep{torne2024rialto,dai2024cousins,li2024simpler,chen2024urdformer}.
Generative scene agents furnish rooms and write task programs from a prompt
\citep{yang2024holodeck,wang2024gensim,wang2024robogen}. A wet-lab station
starts from neither. Its instruments are specific products---a burette with a
working stopcock, an oven whose door travels 180$^{\circ}$---named by a
protocol rather than seen in a scan. Laboratory robotics has policies and
simulators, but no compiler between the notebook and the scene.

Lab2Sim is that compiler, run as an agent (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}). Text,
image, and video enter as one request. The agent searches for the real device
and keeps its manufacturer evidence; writes an asset specification that fixes
envelope, joints, grasp regions, and placement surfaces; iterates a USD
candidate with a Blender skill until it matches; and hands it to
ConvertAsset, whose admission places it in an Isaac laboratory
\citep{nvidia2024isaac}. The specification is the agent's central artifact:
everything upstream is evidence for it, everything downstream is checked
against it.

We make three claims. The compiler runs end to end on real instruments. Its
laboratories span different rooms and manipulation verbs
(Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}). And the task-critical features of an instrument
survive from evidence to specification to admitted USD, which we show on the
OVEN~125 and a traditional burette (Figure~\ref{fig:real-spec}).
